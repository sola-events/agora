Beyond Persistence: Reconceiving Immortality in Homer and Plato’s Phaedo

17 September 2026 · 3.30–5pm · 20-4

RSVP

Abstract

In Aristophanes’ Frogs (118-24), Dionysus inquires of Herakles the quickest path to Hades, to which Herakles responds: ‘hang yourself!’. The question of how to attain immortality seems to admit an analogously succinct response: ‘don’t die!’. After all, it is surely common sense that immortality is exhausted by perpetual personal persistence. But if we bring such logic to an ancient Greek context, the results are likely to surprise. Not only do the Greeks withhold attributions of immortality to entities which exist forever, they extend immortality even to beings for whom death remains an inevitability.

In this paper, I look to the Homeric epics and Plato’s Phaedo to reconstruct a conception of an immortal existence which differs, often radically, from contemporary notions. I argue that, for Homer and Plato, at least, the primary consideration in attributions of immortality is not personal persistence, but rather ‘godlikeness’; to be immortal is understood by reference to an identifiably divine mode of existence. And, as Plato took up and transformed conventional thinking, the relevant consideration in assessing godlikeness came to focus less to the duration of one’s existence, and more to the quality – and especially ethical quality – of this existence.

I conclude that this ancient conception remains implicit in contemporary thought about immortality. And as technological developments enable people to treat perpetual personal persistence as a credible aspiration, Greek reflections raise pressing practical question. Regardless of whether we can live forever, would it be worth it?

Bio

Abe Roth is a Professor in the Philosophy Department at Ohio State University. He received a Ph.D. from Princeton in 1996, and was on the faculty in Philosophy at UCLA from 1998-2002, and at the University of Illinois at Chicago from 2002-2006. He works mainly in the philosophy of action (especially shared agency and collective intentionality), and related areas of epistemology, mind, moral psychology, ethics, and social ontology. He has published in various places, including The Philosophical Review, Noûs, Ethics, Oxford Studies in Agency and Responsibility, Philosophy and Phenomenological Research, and Philosophical Studies.
