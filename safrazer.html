"Darkness enough": A Phenomenology of Night in Thomas Hardy’s The Return of the Native

10 September 2026 · 3.30–5pm · 20-5

Abstract

My subject in this paper is the narrative and ontological implications of the representation of nightscapes in Thomas Hardy’s The Return of the Native (1878). The Return is notable amongst Hardy’s novels for the sheer pitch and duration of its night-time scenes; so much of the novel takes place in the dark, its human characters contending anxiously with an environment the features of which undergo diurnal obliteration. Following the consequences of Hardy’s night-time metaphors I propose to treat the textual field as a medium, one that the novelist finds particularly generative of poetry and character. In doing so, my especial focus will be Eustacia Vye, the novel’s disenchanted heroine. As the apotheosis of Egdon heath’s reality, Eustacia, as I will argue, has a signifying force that puts pressure on her characterisation, a pressure that plays out most dramatically at the level of sensory perception, especially vision: so voluptuously does her figure exceed the orthodox morality of the social world she finds herself trapped within, that the radical figurative energy she both produces and absorbs signals a crisis of representation. It is that imagistic instability and its textual implications that interest me here; and it is Eustacia’s intimacy with the night-drenched landscape of Edgon that most evocatively tells of the trouble that disturbs the unity of her image.

My broader contention in this paper is that Eustacia’s image, always tentative, is slowly unmade over the course of the novel. Drawing on Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s concept of “flesh,” I will propose an analogous elemental force apparent in The Return’s topography, the very mass from which Eustacia emerges and to which she returns in death. Doing so will allow me to read her habits of percipience and her movements across the heath as an active resistance toward figuration. These adumbrations of sensory life, I will argue, in turn reveal something further about The Return’s structuration of consciousness and the permeable borders of body, mind, and world.

Bio

Sophie Alexandra Frazer's research takes place at the intersection of literature and philosophy, with a keen interest in phenomenology of the body. Under the general aegis of phenomenological studies she works on literature (particularly the nineteenth-century novel) that explores states of heightened or extreme feeling and affect, such as desire, disgust, pain, anxiety and terror. She reads literature with an eye to its theoretical and philosophical illuminations. Her current book project is a study of the experience of pain in the fiction of George Eliot and Thomas Hardy, read alongside Benedict de Spinoza's theory of the affects. She has an abiding fascination with twentieth-century French phenomenology, especially Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Emmanuel Levinas, and Simone de Beauvoir.
