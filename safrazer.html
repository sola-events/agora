The orb of my life is not to be so rounded”: The Ambivalence of the Bildungsroman in Charlotte Brontë’s Villette.

4 September 2026 · 3.30–5pm · 20-4

RSVP

Abstract

Charlotte Brontë’s final, desolate novel Villette (1853), often named as her “mature” masterpiece, is a narrative of exile and displacement: one woman’s flight from her home in England to a radically uncertain future in a foreign city. A distinctly phenomenological novelist, in Villette Brontë maps the affective crises of exile and aloneness onto the transmogrifications of sensory life. In this paper, I address Brontë’s ambivalent Bildung and its complex disavowal of orthodox novelistic form. Avowedly ambivalent about her progress in the world, protagonist Lucy Snowe shrewdly repudiates material and worldly success, acting even with nonchalance about her very survival. At the same time, the heroine’s lovelessness is felt as a crisis so acute that it threatens the teleological drive of realist plotting, undermining any possibility of characterological progress —- at least according to the genre’s well-established conventions. In this way, Brontë emphasises both the temptations and perils of conforming to the conventional plot of female development, eschewing marriage for her heroine in preference for an enigmatic and haunting conclusion. I shall ask whether there can be a bildungsroman of regression, an alternative to the two conventional fates of the Victorian female character: between economic, social, and romantic success, on the one hand, and death or ignominy, on the other?

Drawing on the phenomenology of Maurice Merleau-Ponty, I will read Villette as a novel that thematizes its own hauntedness and dis-ease, of painful non-coincidence, addressing what Terry Castle has described as the “ghostification” of the psyche; for if the Victorian realist text tropes on the uncanniness of thought itself, then Villette must surely be its paradigmatic expression.

Bio

Sophie Alexandra Frazer's research takes place at the intersection of literature and philosophy, with a keen interest in phenomenology of the body. Under the general aegis of phenomenological studies she works on literature (particularly the nineteenth-century novel) that explores states of heightened or extreme feeling and affect, such as desire, disgust, pain, anxiety and terror. She reads literature with an eye to its theoretical and philosophical illuminations. Her current book project is a study of the experience of pain in the fiction of George Eliot and Thomas Hardy, read alongside Benedict de Spinoza's theory of the affects. She has an abiding fascination with twentieth-century French phenomenology, especially Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Emmanuel Levinas, and Simone de Beauvoir.
